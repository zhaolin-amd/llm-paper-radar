* The Problem River

When a model works through a long chain of reasoning, the things it "holds in mind" keep piling up — and the GPU memory those memories occupy will eventually overflow. So everyone has been grappling with the same question: keep only a portion of those memories and discard the rest — but how do you discard without throwing away what truly matters?

Five generations of answers grew progressively more sophisticated: first "keep whatever gets recalled most often," then "keep whatever was queried most recently," then "keep whatever isn't redundant," then "keep whatever carries the heaviest information load," then "keep whatever the position-distance curve says to keep." Each generation was a little smarter than the last, and also a little more complicated. Until someone asked the most naïve question of all: does all this cleverness actually help? If you flip a coin to decide what to discard, how much worse would you be?

The answer was startling — barely worse at all. This river starts from "blind guessing won't work, you must score," winds through five loops, and ultimately discovers that as long as you protect one thing (the prompt itself), random guessing is sufficient for everything else. This paper-river traces how those five loops were wound, and why the path circles back to where it began.

* Evolutionary Narrative

** 2023 | StreamingLLM: No Scoring — Survive on Positional Rules Alone

*** What Problem It Saw

Large models needed to support infinitely long streaming conversations (e.g., a customer-service bot chatting all day); the KV cache cannot grow without bound. The most direct fix is a sliding window — keep only the most recent tokens. But once the window slides past the beginning, the model collapses and outputs gibberish.

*** Its Solution

The authors discovered a curious phenomenon: no matter how the window slides, as long as the first few tokens are welded in place and never deleted, the model stays stable — even if those first tokens carry no meaningful content. These tokens act as an "attention sink": softmax must route surplus attention somewhere, and those early positions happen to absorb it. The scheme therefore became: weld the opening few tokens + slide a window over the most recent content.

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

This is a purely geometric/statistical phenomenon with no connection to content importance. It solved "infinite streaming without collapse," but did not solve "how to pick the truly important content from a fixed-length context within a tight budget" — in a long document, a few crucial sentences in the middle would be swept away without hesitation by a pure window. That gap — how to select by content — was left for the next thread.

** 2023 | H2O: The Scoring Era Opens — Keep Whatever Gets Recalled Most

*** What Problem It Saw

StreamingLLM's window is a positional rule that ignores content. But in real text, important tokens (the paper calls them "heavy hitters" — key entities mentioned repeatedly) should be remembered and should not disappear just because the window slides past them.

*** Its Solution

Maintain a running ledger for every cached token: from the moment it enters the cache, accumulate all the attention it receives from subsequent queries. The tokens with the highest ledger scores survive; the rest are evicted. This is "let history prove your importance."

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

Cumulative scoring does capture "repeatedly referenced" content. But the ledger has a natural bias: the earlier a token arrived, the longer it has had to accumulate score — just like an old post naturally gathers more upvotes. Content that becomes critical only later in the reasoning chain may never catch up in time. The ledger also requires continuous maintenance throughout generation. This "earlier arrivals have an unfair advantage, latecomers can't catch up" flaw was the target of the next paper.

** 2024 | SnapKV: Ignore Full History — Vote with Only the Most Recent Window

*** What Problem It Saw

H2O requires maintaining a ledger from start to finish: expensive, and biased toward early tokens. In practice, the typical scenario is a long prompt followed by generation — at that point there is no "generation history" to accumulate from, so the ledger has nowhere to start.

*** Its Solution

SnapKV shifts perspective: instead of "how much has this token accumulated in the past," look at where the most recent queries in a small observation window at the end of the prompt are actually attending. These attention patterns are shown to be stable predictors of what content should be retained going forward — essentially letting the final few sentences "vote" on everything that came before them with their gaze.

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

This avoids H2O-style full-history accumulation and eliminates the "earlier is always better" bias — only the most recent, most representative queries are consulted. But reasoning models have a property SnapKV did not account for: they continuously restate and self-check ("let me verify once more…", "in other words…"). These repetitions are highly similar to one another, so the attention vote keeps landing on the same type of information again and again; the budget fills up with multiple copies of the same thing, leaving no room for genuinely diverse content. This vulnerability — "scoring for importance without penalizing redundancy" — became the entry point for the next paper.

** 2025 | R-KV: Importance Alone Is Not Enough — It Must Also Be Non-Redundant

*** What Problem It Saw

SnapKV's score answers "was this position attended to?" but not "does this position say the same thing as something already kept?" Reasoning chains contain copious self-restatement, so several positions score highly even though they are merely different phrasings of the same idea — keeping any one of them would be fine, but keeping all of them wastes budget.

*** Its Solution

On top of SnapKV's importance score, add a redundancy penalty: compute the cosine similarity between a candidate key and the already-selected keys; the more similar, the heavier the deduction. The scoring formula shifts from "does anyone care about this passage?" to "does anyone care about this passage, and has no one said it before?"

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

This directly targets the "repetitive self-verification" characteristic of reasoning chains and delivers immediate gains — the same budget now covers a more diverse range of information rather than several near-identical self-checks. But its foundation is still "how similar are keys to one another," which uses query-side similarity as a proxy for information content. Similar key vectors do not imply that the corresponding values carry equal weight — the thread of "look at values, not keys" was left for the next paper.

** 2026.06 | VaSE: Stop Counting Queries — Measure What Is Heavy

*** What Problem It Saw

Every preceding method scores by examining the relationship between keys and queries (how many times did anyone look at this?). But the component that actually feeds the model's output computation is the value. The authors identified a statistical regularity: value norms (intuitively, how much information a memory carries) are very unevenly distributed — a small number of positions have norms far exceeding the rest. If those high-norm values are mistakenly evicted, the loss far exceeds evicting several "frequently queried but content-light" keys.

*** Its Solution

Switch the scoring criterion to value norm: positions with large norms are pre-allocated slots and protected first. Additionally, a degree of random sampling is introduced to prevent fully deterministic ranking from repeatedly stumbling on borderline cases.

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

Shifting the signal from "query frequency on the key side" to "content magnitude on the value side" captures something none of the preceding generations directly quantified. But it still relies on "the most recent queries in an observation window" to trigger scoring — and that window has a geometric vulnerability: RoPE applies rotational encoding to every position's query and key vectors, so the later the position, the more the query vector's orientation has rotated. The few "most recent queries" in the observation window are systematically pointing in a different direction from earlier queries. Using a handful of rotated arrows to represent the global picture is inherently unstable. This "RoPE distorts the observation window" problem was directly confronted by the next paper.

** 2026.04 | TriAttention: Retreat to Pre-Rotation Space and Score by Positional Distance

*** What Problem It Saw

Whether H2O, SnapKV, R-KV, or VaSE, scoring always requires live queries to cast votes or compute similarity in real time. But RoPE continuously rotates query vectors with position, so the most recent queries in the observation window have already turned to a different orientation than earlier queries; using them to judge "which earlier keys deserve to be kept" is like reading a compass in a magnetically disturbed field — the reading is unreliable. And the distortion only worsens as generation grows longer.

*** Its Solution

TriAttention observes that in the pre-RoPE space, Q and K vectors are remarkably stable: they cluster tightly around a few fixed centers without drifting with position. Building on this stability, a "distance–attention" trigonometric curve can be calibrated offline for each attention head, describing approximately how much attention a query would pay to a key at a given positional distance. With this curve, scoring a key requires no live queries at all — just plug in the distance between the key's position and the current generation position.

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

This completely sidesteps the "observation window distortion" problem. Scoring becomes a deterministic computation based purely on positional distance and outperforms all prior methods across several models and tasks. The cost is that the "scoring" mechanism has now become an elaborate system of offline calibration, per-head differentiation, and trigonometric functions incorporating density statistics — considerable complexity. And it is still a "score," still assuming that "positional distance" reliably predicts "future importance." The target paper's next move is to ask: across this entire chain from H2O to TriAttention, with each generation growing more ingenious, how much accuracy do the scores themselves actually contribute?

** 2026.09 | Random Attention: Strip Away All Scoring and Return to the Start — With an Explanation

*** What Problem It Saw

The authors performed a near-ground-level ablation: they unified the "how to handle the prompt" rule across SnapKV, R-KV, and VaSE — regardless of how each method scores, the prompt (the question itself) is always fully protected and never entered into eviction competition. The result: once this rule was unified, the accuracy gaps among the methods nearly disappeared entirely. In other words, what past generations had been competing on — "whose scores are more accurate" — was largely measuring "who happened to protect more of the prompt," with little dependence on the sophistication of the scores themselves.

*** Its Solution

Eliminate the scoring step entirely. The policy collapses to two rules: first, the prompt is always fully retained, not a single token subject to eviction; second, the remaining reasoning-chain content is sampled uniformly at random, independently per attention head, with no scores computed.

*** Why It Worked (and What Trap It Left)

This works because reasoning chains carry two layers of natural redundancy. The first is textual: the model continuously restates "what I still need to use right now" (exactly the phenomenon R-KV tried but didn't fully articulate). The second is structural: a Transformer has many attention heads, each storing its own complete copy of the reasoning chain; for a piece of content to be truly lost, it must be randomly dropped in every single head simultaneously — with independent per-head random sampling, this natural redundancy is exploited to the fullest. As a result, "what needs to be kept" almost always survives in at least one head, with no need for precise scoring to identify it. The paper also honestly marks the boundary: if a single critical fact appears only once in the reasoning chain and is never restated (e.g., a one-time passcode), the random policy can genuinely lose it, and a signal-driven scoring method would theoretically still have value — it's just that in real reasoning traces, content that is stated once and never mentioned again is exceedingly rare.

* Insight

On the surface, this river is about "how to score and evict KV cache entries" — five generations of methods growing ever more refined, almost like a craft unto itself. But stepping back, the real transformation is this: everyone was optimizing "how to rank content within the reasoning chain," without first asking "does evicting the wrong content actually cause catastrophic failure?" The answer turns out to be: evicting the prompt is catastrophic; evicting reasoning-chain content is mostly absorbed by natural redundancy. The progress each generation of scoring methods made was, in large part, inadvertently — and in different ways — doing a better job of protecting the prompt. The growing complexity of the scores happened to conceal the simpler, more fundamental act of "just protect the prompt."

This is also a research pattern worth remembering: once a field is framed by an implicit premise ("you must score to select correctly"), subsequent work continuously refines within that premise until someone runs the ablation of "strip the premise entirely and see how much gap remains" — and discovers the premise never held. The most likely next frontier is the boundary Random Attention itself drew: the rare critical fact that is stated once and never restated (a passcode, a one-time instruction) is precisely the blind spot of random strategies. How to cover that blind spot with an extremely lightweight signal targeted only at such content — without retreating to scoring everything all over again — will be the next battleground.

* Takeaway

Scoring is not the only option; "should this be unconditionally protected?" is the prior question. When facing a "select / evict" problem, rather than immediately designing a smarter scoring function, ask first: does this system contain content that, once lost, is irreplaceable (non-regenerable inputs), and other content that, even if lost, can almost certainly be reconstructed through some other path (redundant process)? Weld the irreplaceable content in place unconditionally; for the rest, the simplest random strategy is usually sufficient. This means that when designing any resource-constrained, trade-off-forcing system — cache eviction, log sampling, feature selection — the first step is not to tune a scoring formula. It is to draw the boundary between "what is non-regenerable" and "what is inherently redundant."
