* The River of the Problem

A large model is first "slimmed down" (structural compression, e.g. 120B → 60B) and then "squashed flat" (4-bit quantization). After both cuts, the model's reasoning, math, and coding capabilities are very likely to degrade. How do you recover those lost points without paying an astronomical training bill?

The difficulty is that conventional "score recovery" methods all assume compression happened only once — either quantization alone with no structural pruning, or structural pruning alone with no quantization. Once both cuts land together, the question of "who should be the teacher" becomes murky: the structurally-slimmed bfloat16 checkpoint sitting in your hands — does it even qualify as a "trustworthy teacher"? Every paper in this river is answering the question "what to use as a teacher, and what relationship should the teacher and student maintain," inching toward the answer step by step.

*Honest caveat*: Jacob 2018 and Polino 2018 are not in a strict citation chain — they are two parallel branches that appeared in the same year ("how to train a model while tolerating quantization error" and "how to use distillation to compensate for quantization capacity loss"). This paper genuinely inherits the latter's idea of "distillation to compensate for capacity loss"; the former supplies the operational paradigm of "quantization-aware training" itself. This provenance line traces back to 2018, covering 5 layers, reaching the foundational works of the field.

* Evolutionary Narrative

** 2018 | Jacob et al.: Rehearse quantization during training — don't be blindsided at deployment

*** What problem it saw

The early approach was post-training quantization (PTQ): train the model to completion, then round floats to integers. But the dynamic range varies enormously across layers, and outliers (a few extremely large values) cause quantization error to amplify, badly degrading accuracy.

*** Its solution

Since quantization will happen at deployment, rehearse it during training: insert "fake quantization" nodes into the compute graph so that weights and activations are simulated-quantized and then restored at training time, forcing the model to absorb this error in advance. By the time the model is actually deployed on integer hardware, the error has already been "digested."

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

The logic is straightforward: rather than having a student encounter the real exam format for the first time on graduation day, use that format throughout practice sessions. But the method requires access to the original training data and the compute budget to run training again — no problem for the small CV models of that era, but it buried a trap: if the model is too large to retrain cheaply, or the data is simply unavailable, this approach falls apart. That is exactly the problem the LLM era would face a few years later.

** 2018 | Polino, Pascanu & Alistarh: Enduring quantization error alone is not enough — bring in an uncut teacher to help

*** What problem it saw

Quantization is fundamentally a capacity reduction — fewer representable values means a smaller model. If this reduced network is left to learn only from hard labels on its own, its recovery ceiling is capped by that reduced capacity, and it cannot climb beyond it.

*** Its solution

Introduce knowledge distillation: find a large, uncompressed network to serve as the "teacher," and let the quantized small network be the "student." Instead of learning only from hard labels, the student mimics the teacher's output distribution (soft labels). The teacher's knowledge effectively props up the student against its capacity bottleneck.

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

The teacher's output distribution encodes far richer information than "one correct answer" (e.g., "this example looks like A but somewhat like B"), and this richness can compensate for the expressiveness lost through capacity reduction. But like Jacob 2018, this paper still assumes "you can afford to retrain with the full training dataset" — an assumption that would soon buckle under the weight of models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters.

** 2023 | Frantar et al. (GPTQ): Training is too expensive — skip it entirely and "precisely cut" mathematically

*** What problem it saw

GPT-scale models have tens to hundreds of billions of parameters; even a single retraining run costs a fortune. Prior PTQ methods could only compress to moderate bit-widths, or were far too slow at hundred-billion-parameter scale (the paper estimates that applying earlier weight-by-weight optimization to GPT-175B would take six months).

*** Its solution

GPTQ does not train at all — it relies on mathematics: using approximate second-order information (Hessian), it quantizes weights layer by layer, block by block, while simultaneously adjusting the remaining unquantized weights in the same layer to compensate for the error already introduced. The entire process finishes in a few GPU-hours for hundred-billion-parameter models.

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

It eliminates the option of retraining entirely, replacing the training loop with pure mathematical optimization, cutting the cost from "astronomical" to "a few hours." But there is no free lunch: the lower the bit-width (4-bit and below), the more the error compensation from purely mathematical cutting falls short — especially on detail-sensitive tasks like reasoning and code. This opened the door again to the question of whether some form of training should be reintroduced.

** 2023 | Liu et al. (LLM-QAT): PTQ can't hold at 4-bit, but LLMs can't use real data for QAT the old way either

*** What problem it saw

PTQ methods like GPTQ work reasonably well at 8-bit but start dropping noticeably below 4-bit. Returning to QAT (the Jacob 2018 approach) could theoretically recover more accuracy, but LLM pretraining corpora are massive, complex, and often inaccessible or prohibitively expensive to process — the old path is blocked.

*** Its solution

Since real data is unavailable, let the model generate its own training data: sample text sequences from the pretrained model itself, use them as the distillation data source, and combine this with quantization-aware training. No external corpus required. KV-cache quantization is also incorporated.

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

Text generated by the model naturally fits its own output distribution; distilling with this data is more "on-target" than forcing a mismatched external corpus. But LLM-QAT still trains under the original task loss (cross-entropy) — meaning that if the model underwent complex post-training beyond pretraining (SFT, RL alignment), QAT's hard cross-entropy correction could overwrite the behavioral habits that RL painstakingly instilled. This problem would fully ignite two years later as post-training pipelines grew ever more complex.

** 2026 | NVIDIA (QAD): Stop forcing the student to practice from "answer keys" — let it copy the teacher's distribution instead

*** What problem it saw

By 2026, mainstream LLM training had become a multi-stage pipeline: pretraining → SFT → RL → model merging. NVIDIA found that applying traditional QAT (retraining with cross-entropy) to such models, the task loss would destroy the specialized capabilities that the RL stage had carefully developed — in some cases, the QAT-trained model performed worse than doing nothing (pure PTQ).

*** Its solution

Replace the QAT task loss with KL-divergence distillation toward a frozen, bfloat16 full-precision teacher — this is QAD (Quantization-Aware Distillation). The student only needs to match the teacher's output distribution; it does not need to relearn what the "correct answer" is.

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

Mimicking a distribution is far gentler than mimicking labels — the teacher's distribution already encodes the behavioral preferences shaped by RL alignment, and the student learns those without overwriting them. NVIDIA also demonstrated that this method requires very little training data in terms of quantity or quality. But QAD carries a hidden assumption: teacher and student must "look the same" (same architecture), because it assumes the teacher is simply the pre-quantization bfloat16 version of the same model. This holds when only quantization is applied; but the moment the model has first been structurally compressed (e.g., 120B → 60B), the question of who the "pre-quantization bfloat16 version" really is becomes a new problem.

** 2026 | Ryskulov et al. (this paper, QAH): That bfloat16 checkpoint is itself a forgery — don't copy a copy

*** What problem it saw

When a model has first been structurally compressed (120B → 60B) and is now about to be quantized, who is the "bfloat16 teacher" that QAD needs? The only ready-made candidate is the "post-compression, distillation-recovered 60B bfloat16 checkpoint." But the authors make a sharp observation: this checkpoint was never independently trained — it is itself "an approximation recovered from the original model via distillation," inheriting the capability losses that compression introduced. Using this approximation as a teacher to train the quantized student is like tracing a photocopy of a photocopy — the student's accuracy ceiling is pinned from the start at the level of that approximation.

*** Its solution

QAH bypasses this "second-hand teacher" entirely. The final 4-bit student model distills directly from the original, fully uncompressed 120B full-precision model. It does not matter that the teacher and student have completely different architectures — the supervision signal is only the teacher's output distribution, which is architecture-agnostic.

*** Why it worked (and what trap it set)

In a word: "cut out the middleman." No matter how well that 60B bfloat16 checkpoint was constructed, it is only a shadow of the original model; learning directly from the original raises the student's reachable ceiling — in the paper, the 4-bit, 60B student matches or exceeds its "official" bfloat16 counterpart on 7 out of 9 benchmarks, while using only one-quarter the memory of the original 120B teacher. And because the task loss need not be relearned, training is 7× faster than QAT and more stable. The authors also candidly acknowledge several gaps left unfilled: the method has only been validated on the GPT-OSS model family and the MXFP4 format; the compression operator itself is a proprietary black box; it remains to be shown whether results hold when switching to other structural compression methods such as SliceGPT or layer pruning. They also note an unexpected finding — different distributed training backends produce reproducible quality differences — which may itself be the next worthwhile excavation site.

* Frontier Extensions

This paper was published on 2026-08-21; at retrieval time (2026-08-26) only five days had passed, and no subsequent papers directly critiquing or improving QAH were found. The open problems the authors themselves list — the generality of the compression operator, and the quality differences caused by distributed training backends — are very likely the natural directions this river flows next. As of now, QAH is the most recent link in this "joint compression + quantization recovery" problem line.

* Insight

On the surface this river is about "how quantization-recovery techniques grow stronger generation by generation," but what is truly changing is one thing: *the definition of "teacher" is continuously being reexamined — each generation asks "does the teacher I'm currently using actually deserve that title?"*

- Jacob 2018 makes "one's future self" the teacher (simulating future error);
- Polino 2018 first realizes that self-reliance is insufficient — an external teacher that has never been cut is needed;
- GPTQ abandons "training" altogether, sidestepping the teacher question entirely;
- LLM-QAT, unable to obtain real data, falls back to having the model generate data for itself — the model becomes its own teacher's shadow;
- QAD realizes that "the task loss" itself is teaching the student bad habits, so it replaces "the answer key" with "the teacher's distribution";
- QAH is the first to ask: does the bfloat16 teacher I have in hand actually qualify as a teacher? — discovering that it is itself a student (an approximation recovered via distillation), and so skipping past it to learn directly from "the grandparent."

The most likely next step is probably not finding an ever-better teacher, but rather beginning to interrogate "how large an architectural gap between student and teacher can there be before the distillation signal stops transmitting effectively" — because QAH has already pushed "the teacher can look completely different from the student" to a new extreme (120B MoE teaching 60B MoE, with structural compression in between). Naturally, someone will soon ask: how far can that gap be stretched? How large a capacity chasm can pure output-distribution distillation actually bridge?

* Takeaway

The lesson this evolutionary line offers to researchers is: *when a method hits a wall, resist the urge to immediately tune hyperparameters — step back and check whether the "authoritative source" you tacitly accepted is itself standing on solid ground*. When QAT stalled, everyone was adjusting loss functions, data, and training stability; QAH's insight was instead to retreat one step and question the legitimacy of the bfloat16 checkpoint that everyone took for granted.

The practical implication: whenever you are doing any "use A to recover/correct B" work, first ask — is this A clean and independently generated, or is it itself "distilled/approximated" from something earlier? If it is the latter, the ceiling of whatever you recover is pinned from the start at that approximation's level. Bypassing it and going straight to the true source may be more effective than continuing to refine the recovery algorithm itself.
