* The Problem River

Speculative decoding aims to let large models "output several tokens at once" by having a cheap draft model guess first, then letting the large model verify in a single parallel pass. But when inputs stretch to tens of thousands of tokens — long-document summarization, multi-turn agents — that "cheap guess" either guesses poorly or guesses slowly. Why can't we have both?

The difficulty is this: accurate guessing requires the draft model to "see deeply and see far," understanding which information across the entire history is relevant to the next token. But the farther it sees, the larger the KV cache it must read at every step, making each draft step slower and eating back all the time speculative decoding saved. Generation after generation of methods has been pulled between these two poles: either reduce what the draft "sees," or reduce what the draft "carries." Almost no one has asked — can the draft model compress its "seen history" ahead of time and carry a compact version forward?

* Evolutionary Narrative

** 2023 | Speculative Decoding: Small model drafts, large model verifies in parallel — lossless speedup

*** What problem it saw

Autoregressive decoding is every large model's fate: compute one token before computing the next; GPU parallelism sits largely idle. Speeding this up while preserving the model's output distribution — no "cutting corners" — seemed impossible.

*** Its solution

Let a cheap small model produce a few candidate tokens (a draft); the large model needs only a single parallel forward pass to verify them all. Correct tokens are accepted directly; at the first wrong token, generation resumes from there. The accept/reject rule is mathematically proven not to alter the large model's original output distribution — parallelism is exploited for free, with no accuracy loss.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

It works because whenever the draft's guesses are mostly correct, one verification pass from the large model "confirms" several tokens at once — free tokens in effect. But the new pit is right there: where do you find a small model that is "cheap enough and accurate enough"? Forcing an awkwardly-sized model into service can be slower than not using speculative decoding at all. This "pairing difficulty" directly spawned the next wave of work.

** 2024 | Medusa: Stop looking for an external small model — attach prediction heads directly onto the large model

*** What problem it saw

Training or sourcing a compatible standalone draft model for every large model is expensive engineering, and the resulting speed ratio may still fall short.

*** Its solution

Attach several lightweight MLP "heads" in parallel to the large model's second-to-last layer features, each head responsible for predicting the token k steps into the future. No standalone draft model is needed at all — the draft lives on the large model itself.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

It eliminates the pairing headache. The cost is that each head guesses in isolation — given the same feature, if the true continuation could reasonably be "I have always" or "I always," each head guesses independently, making contradictions easy and multi-step accuracy hard to raise.

** 2024 | EAGLE (ICML): Make the draft autoregress at the feature level instead of having heads guess independently

*** What problem it saw

Medusa's heads share no information, so feature-level prediction is inherently ambiguous — given the same feature, which token comes next is uncertain, and multi-step guesses become increasingly incoherent.

*** Its solution

Feed the "already-guessed token" back as input, making the draft model advance step by step in an autoregressive manner at the feature level — this step's output is conditioned on the previous step's guess, resolving ambiguity. The draft still reuses the large model's top-layer features; it simply trains one additional lightweight autoregressive layer on top.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

Conditioning on the previously guessed token turns Medusa's ambiguous parallel guessing into causally chained sequential guessing, dramatically improving acceptance rates. But the new pit: the reused top-layer feature is fundamentally built to serve one job — predicting the very next token — and its information content is bounded. No matter how much training data is poured in, draft capacity quickly plateaus (it stops scaling).

** 2025 | EAGLE-3 (NeurIPS): Stop predicting features; predict tokens directly — and look deeper

*** What problem it saw

The top-layer feature EAGLE reuses encodes only the information needed for the next token; using it to predict tokens two or three steps ahead is a stretch. Worse, pouring in more training data doesn't increase the information that layer can provide — the draft model simply stops learning.

*** Its solution

Abandon the "predict features, then look up the vocabulary" approach; predict tokens directly instead. At the same time, stop looking at only the large model's last layer — fuse shallow, middle, and deep layer semantic features and feed them all to the draft model, giving it access to both surface-form and semantic information simultaneously.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

Multi-layer feature fusion lets the draft model improve continuously as training data grows — a scaling law for speculative decoding is found for the first time — and short-context speedup ratios improve noticeably. However, none of this touches "how should a draft model handle long contexts" — the draft is still an autoregressive model whose own KV cache grows unboundedly. Once inputs extend to tens of thousands of tokens, the problem resurfaces in a different form.

** 2025 | LongSpec (ACL): The draft model's own cache cannot grow without bound

*** What problem it saw

EAGLE-style draft models carry their own growing KV cache; at long contexts this cache consumes large amounts of VRAM and slows every decoding step. Furthermore, training on short sequences (a few thousand tokens) while inferring on long ones (tens of thousands) causes positional encoding train/inference mismatch, visibly degrading quality.

*** Its solution

Borrow from StreamingLLM's "attention sink" phenomenon — models consistently assign large attention weight to the first few tokens — and pin those tokens as fixed anchor position indices. All other positions receive a random offset, so regardless of context length, the draft model's positional encodings always fall within the range seen during training. Draft KV cache size is designed to be constant, never growing with context length.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

Fixed anchor positions plus a constant cache simultaneously solve both "train/inference positional mismatch" and "VRAM explosion," allowing speculative decoding speedup to persist at long contexts. But the draft backbone is inherited from EAGLE — still that shallow, single-layer autoregressive structure. The capacity bottleneck is never truly addressed: the longer the context, the more complex the history that must be retained, and a shallow draft eventually buckles — acceptance length still falls as context grows.

** 2026-08 | This Paper (MASW): Reclaim both capacity and speed with a strong draft model and learnable compressed memory

*** What problem it saw

The paper presents the contradiction plainly with an empirical figure: an EAGLE-style shallow draft's acceptance length falls monotonically with context length in long-context settings. Switching to a strong standalone draft model with the same architecture as the target model (e.g., a 3B model drafting for an 8B target) keeps acceptance length high throughout — but this strong draft must read its entire accumulated KV history at every step. The cache snowballs, per-step latency grows nearly linearly with context length, and the time saved by speculative decoding is entirely consumed by I/O overhead. In short: LongSpec's path of "controlling cache size" sacrifices the draft's ability to "see deeply"; a "deep-seeing" strong draft loses control of its cache. These are two faces of the same contradiction, and no prior work resolved both simultaneously.

*** Its solution

Keep a strong standalone draft model with more capacity than EAGLE-style architectures, but stop letting it read the full raw KV history. The draft's working memory is split into three parts: the first few "sink" tokens (raw KV, constant size); a small recent sliding window (raw KV, for accurate near-context); and the "distant history" in between — this portion no longer retains raw KV. Instead, at fixed intervals a lightweight, purpose-trained "memory adapter" compresses the information from the current sliding window into a fixed set of "memory slots." Earlier compressed information is passed forward from slot to slot like a relay baton. When decoding, the draft model reads only "sink tokens + local window + memory slots" — a constant-size compact memory rather than the full history. The target model (responsible for final verification) is entirely unaffected: it retains its full KV cache and the standard accept/reject rule, leaving speculative decoding's lossless guarantee intact.

*** Why it works (and what new pit it digs)

The key insight is this: the root of the capacity bottleneck was never "whether to compress history" — it was "what to compress with." Every prior long-context speculative decoding work used human-designed rules to decide which tokens to drop or retain. No one trained a module specifically to learn "how to distill history into a fixed size while preserving information useful for prediction." MASW makes the compression process itself learnable. The draft preserves its "strength" (architectural capacity, plus the remote information carried in compressed memory) while keeping what it reads pinned to a fixed size that never grows with context — latency no longer rises linearly with length. The paper validates on Llama 3.1-8B/70B: draft-side VRAM drops by more than 70%, throughput exceeds standard autoregressive decoding by 2–3× or more, and MASW consistently outperforms both EAGLE-style baselines and the full-KV standalone draft baseline at both target model scales.

New pits are also dug here: the compressed memory is produced by a frozen backbone plus a trained adapter, and the adapter requires an additional training stage with additional data (the paper uses a two-stage training procedure). The tradeoff between compression ratio and fidelity is not yet thoroughly explored. Furthermore, the compressed memory is append-only — once a slot is written, it cannot be revised. If later context contradicts or supersedes an early compressed snapshot (e.g., a topic is reversed), the current design has no mechanism to go back and correct it.

* Frontier Extensions

This paper was published on August 31, 2026, and is the newest link in this evolutionary chain. As of this survey, no follow-up papers explicitly critiquing or improving MASW have been identified. The paper itself acknowledges that "replacing distant raw KV with learnable compressed state" has not been attempted in any prior work — placing it at the current frontier of this line, not yet challenged by successors.

* Insight

What has genuinely changed along this line is not "which model to use as the draft" but rather the evolving answer to "how should the draft model regard history": from "don't look at history at all" (external small model / parallel heads), to "look, but only use one ready-made layer from the large model" (EAGLE / EAGLE-3), to "look, but discard most history with handcrafted rules" (LongSpec's sink + window), and finally to "look, but let a purpose-trained module decide how to compress history" (MASW's learnable memory). Each advance transfers the decision-making authority over "how to compress history" — from human prior judgment (which layer is useful, which tokens to drop) — progressively toward a module that can be trained on data. The most likely next direction is giving the compression module the ability to selectively forget or update: MASW's memory is currently append-only. Once compressed memory can be revised in light of new evidence, this line will likely converge again with research on long-term memory and continual learning.

* Takeaway

This evolutionary line points to a general research pattern: when two metrics of a system (here, "accuracy" and "speed") are persistently in tension, don't rush to find a compromise point between them. First ask — is the "hidden variable" in the middle (here, "how to compress historical information") still being governed by handcrafted rules? If so, replacing it with a learnable module often lifts both metrics simultaneously rather than simply rebalancing a tradeoff. This means that when you encounter your own "speed vs. quality" tension, start by finding the intermediate step held in place by hand-engineered rules, then ask: "Could this step be replaced by a small trained module?"
