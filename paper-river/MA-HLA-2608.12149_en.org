* The River of the Problem

In the hidden states of large language models, a handful of values are always
outrageously large — pull them out and the model collapses. What exactly are
these "nails," and where do they come from? More pressingly: the industry has
started interleaving full-attention layers with cheaper linear-attention layers
to save GPU memory. Do these nails still sit in the same place in the new
architecture, or have they taken on a different shape?

The difficulty is that these outsize values look like anomalies at first glance
— things to be cleaned away — yet every time someone tries to remove them,
model performance crashes. Understanding their role directly determines how to
do quantization compression, how to handle long-context inference, and how to
design the next generation of efficient attention mechanisms without breaking
the model. And because architectures keep changing (from pure Transformers to
hybrid linear attention), that understanding must be re-verified generation by
generation; it cannot simply be carried over unchanged.

* The Evolutionary Narrative

** 2023 | StreamingLLM: Making a Large Model Chat with You Forever — Without Crashing

*** What Problem It Saw

A dialogue agent must keep remembering the conversation history, but every
token's Key/Value occupies GPU memory; the longer the chat, the faster memory
explodes. A natural cost-saving approach is a "sliding window" — keep only the
most recent few turns and discard the old ones. Reasonable in theory, but in
practice: perplexity (ppl) jumps from the tens straight to over five thousand,
and the model starts talking gibberish.

*** Its Solution

The authors did not rush to adjust window size; instead they looked back at
exactly what was being discarded. They found that simply re-inserting the very
first few tokens (even semantically empty ones) into the cache alongside the
recent window immediately brought perplexity from five-thousand-plus back down
to around five. These leading tokens were named *attention sinks* — attention
flows the way water does, always needing somewhere to drain, and regardless of
context those frontmost positions are invariably chosen as the drain.

*** Why It Works (and Where a New Trap Is Buried)

Softmax attention has a mathematical fate: all positional weights must sum to 1.
When most tokens are actually irrelevant to the current prediction, the model
still has to distribute that full budget of 1 somewhere — and it is convenient
to hand it to a globally visible, position-fixed token. This paper clarified
"where attention flows" but left a deeper question unanswered: what is actually
happening inside the hidden states of the token being used as a drain? That
question was left for the next paper.

** 2024 | Massive Activations: Inside the Drain Token, a Few Absurdly Large Numbers Are Hiding

*** What Problem It Saw

StreamingLLM identified which tokens act as drains, but no one had looked
inside those tokens' hidden-state vectors. Studying only attention weights, one
might assume this is purely a routing-layer trick — nobody had verified whether
this special treatment also leaves a numerical imprint deep inside the model.

*** Its Solution

The authors peeled back the hidden states of models like LLaMA layer by layer
and found that across millions of values, only a handful (typically ~4) were
absurdly large — up to a hundred thousand times bigger than the rest — and
barely changed no matter what sentence was fed in. These were named
*massive activations (MA)*. A sharper experiment: forcing these values to zero
immediately crashed the model; replacing them with a fixed mean left the model
entirely unaffected — proving they do not encode "what this sentence says" at
all. They are bias constants self-welded by the model, attached to the tokens
serving as attention sinks.

*** Why It Works (and Where a New Trap Is Buried)

This filled the gap StreamingLLM left: sink tokens are selected not merely by
an attention-weight allocation game, but because their hidden states are loaded
with a few constant bias values that in turn attract attention to cluster around
them, forming a self-reinforcing mechanism. Yet, like StreamingLLM before it,
all of this was observed in Transformers where every layer is a full softmax
attention layer. Nor was any general method offered for conveniently locating
these "big nails" in an arbitrary model.

** 2025 | Systematic Outliers (ICLR 2025): Elevating "Large Numbers" from Individual Phenomenon to Unified Theory and General Ruler

*** What Problem It Saw

Massive Activations had identified only the most extreme individual points in
activation space — the tip of the iceberg — without linking them to equally
outlying values found in weights and attention scores, without explaining why
those particular positions were selected, and without offering a detection
procedure that could be applied uniformly across different models.

*** Its Solution

The authors split the outlier phenomenon into three categories — weight
outliers, activation outliers, and attention outliers — and used theoretical
derivation combined with experiments to show that these form a causal chain:
extreme weight values first generate extreme activation values, which in turn
inflate certain attention scores. The unified name given was *systematic
outliers*. An operational criterion was also provided: simply rank by magnitude;
positions that stand dramatically apart from the rest are where the
outliers/sinks reside.

*** Why It Works (and Where a New Trap Is Buried)

Linking the three types of outliers into a causal chain is far more explanatory
than looking at MA alone — it reveals softmax as the common underlying driver
rather than merely "observing" the phenomenon. However, the criterion carries a
hidden premise: that the model is built entirely from standard softmax
full-attention layers stacked end to end. After 2025, that assumption began to
crack. Industry had started interleaving cheap linear-attention layers with
full-attention layers (HLA) to save memory, and nobody had verified whether the
magnitude-ranking criterion still held in such new architectures. That gap is
exactly what the target paper sets out to fill.

** 2026-08 | Target Paper: Moving the MA Microscope onto the "Cost-Saving New Architecture" HLA

*** What Problem It Saw

HLA is now used at industrial scale — Qwen3-Next, Kimi Linear, Nemotron-H, and
others all interleave full attention with linear attention. Yet all prior
MA/attention-sink theory rested on the assumption that every layer is full
attention. When the authors tested this, they found that An et al.'s
magnitude-ranking detector breaks down in HLA models outright — the top-ranked
positions no longer correspond to the actual sink tokens. With the architecture
changed, the old ruler gives wrong readings, and how HLA reshapes the internal
MA phenomenon is entirely uncharted territory.

*** Its Solution

Since magnitude ranking is unreliable, the authors switched to a tracking
approach that follows the attention-sink token itself rather than relying on
value rankings alone. Using this new ruler, they conducted a systematic sweep
across 5 linear-attention architectures, 6 hybrid configurations, 5 data
domains, and multiple open-source models ranging from 1.2B to 397B parameters.
They found that MA manifests in two regular forms inside HLA: first,
*pre-attention spikes (PAS)* — MA values surge sharply just before entering
each full-attention layer; second, *inter-spike plateaus (ISP)* — the elevated
large values are carried unchanged through the intervening linear-attention
layers until the next full-attention layer. They also ran controlled
pre-training experiments to verify how gating mechanisms modulate these two
forms.

*** Why It Works (and Where a New Trap Is Buried)

This demonstrates that the MA "bias nail" is not the exclusive property of
softmax full attention — in HLA it simply adopts a new mode of existence: full-
attention layers remain the "generators" of MA (spikes emerge here), while
linear-attention layers degrade into mere "carriers" (plateaus transport the
values unchanged). When the proportion of full-attention layers is high enough,
this pattern collapses back into the familiar MA shape seen in standard
Transformers — which goes some way toward explaining why HLA can largely
preserve performance while saving memory. But new questions remain: if
generating MA fundamentally requires at least a small number of full-attention
layers, how do purely linear-attention models (zero full-attention) achieve
comparable stability? That thread has not yet reached its end.

* Frontier Extensions

The paper was published only in August 2026 (submitted just one day prior); no
citations or follow-up work have been found. This is accurately stated: as of
now, this is the latest development on this line of research. The paper itself
identifies two possible next steps — both self-declared open questions, not
speculation introduced here:

1. The paper cites Qiu et al. 2026's "attention-sink-free" gating mechanism,
   which offers an alternative path: rather than continuing to study how MA
   behaves in HLA, one could design attention mechanisms that require no
   sinks/MA in the first place.
2. The paper's own controlled pre-training experiments show that gating strength
   modulates the shape of PAS/ISP, implying that "how to design gating" will
   become the next variable to be systematically studied.

* Insight

The real change running through this line of work is not "yet another defect
discovered." It is a shift in how the community understands outliers/MA/sinks:
from "anomalies to be eliminated" to "necessary mechanisms self-invented by the
model, which must be tracked as architectures evolve." Every time the
architecture changes (full attention → hybrid linear attention), the old
detection tools and theoretical explanations must be re-verified from scratch;
they cannot be assumed to transfer — that is the paper's most important
methodological warning. Efficiency-driven architectural innovation will
continually overturn "seemingly mature" mechanistic understanding, forcing the
community to return again and again to the question of "what is this thing,
really?" The most likely next step is to treat "the shape of MA" as a knob that
can be actively designed — not explaining MA after the fact, but deciding at
the architecture-design stage whether to allow it to exist, and in what form.

* Implications

The lesson this evolutionary line offers for research practice is: whenever a
theory or tool that has been repeatedly validated in one setting relies on an
underlying assumption that has now been broken by a new architecture (here, the
assumption that "every layer is softmax full attention" is shattered by HLA),
it is worth going back to re-verify the phenomenon directly. The old conclusions
may not be wrong, but their domain of applicability may have quietly shrunk.
This means: at any intersection of "new architecture + old theory," resist the
urge to apply the old detection method or evaluation metric immediately. First
ask: "What architectural assumption does this method silently take for granted,
and does the new architecture still satisfy it?"
