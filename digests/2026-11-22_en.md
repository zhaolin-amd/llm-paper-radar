#### Summary
Indexers for top-k sparse attention are typically distilled at full serving length, making training expensive for long contexts. This work identifies RoPE, not degraded content matching, as the primary cause of poor length generalization when indexers are trained on short sequences, and proposes position-skipped distillation: short training sequences are assigned position IDs with a randomly placed gap, so neither the indexer nor the frozen teacher processes more than the short training length. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite and Qwen3-30B-A3B, an 8K-distilled indexer matches a 32K-distilled one to within ~0.5 perplexity at 32K, and matches a 64K-distilled indexer at 64K with ~6x less training compute per token. Additionally, indexer keys can be quantized to 3–4 bits without retraining; at 4 bits, perplexity changes by at most 0.05 and a fused integer kernel achieves 1.6–2.2x faster scans than half precision.

- 🎯 Position-skipped distillation inserts random gaps in position IDs, enabling 8K-sequence training to generalize to long contexts via RoPE-aware gap injection
- 📊 On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, 8K-distilled indexer matches 64K-distilled quality at 64K with ~6x less training compute per token
- 🔧 3–4 bit quantization of indexer keys requires no retraining; at 4 bits PPL shift ≤0.05 and fused integer kernel is 1.6–2.2x faster than half precision
- ⚠️ On Llama-3.1-8B, trails the 32K-distilled run by 0.6 perplexity; gains are primarily in training cost rather than quality

#### 🧭 Why this paper
**Survey / methodology & comparison** · topic 4/5 · practicality 3/5 · composite 7/10 · This paper does not propose a new quantization algorithm; instead it systematically measures the output layer of quantized models to reveal the 'silenced token' phenomenon: certain tokens can no longer be produced by a greedy decoder after quantization. The study covers 3 models at 8/4/3-bit precision and finds that non-Latin characters (Korean, Chinese, Thai, Arabic, Hebrew) in multilingual vocabularies are silenced at significantly higher rates, providing an actionable measurement perspective beyond standard PPL/accuracy. It falls under the category of compression-effect measurement work and is of direct reference value to engineers deploying multilingual quantized LLMs. However, there are no standard downstream benchmarks such as MMLU/GSM8K, the largest tested model is only 1B parameters, and practicality is limited by the absence of concrete optimization solutions—only a diagnostic tool is provided.

#### Summary
Quantization is typically evaluated via perplexity and task accuracy, but these metrics miss a specific degradation: tokens that become unreachable by a greedy decoder after quantization—termed 'silenced tokens'. Three complementary checks are proposed—a winning-direction certificate (safe), a proximity upper bound (trapped), and an exact match (never wins)—forming a certified interval estimate of the silenced-token count that collapses to an exact count in nearly all settings down to 3 bits. Across three open models, silencing appears at 8-bit precision on large-vocabulary models, reaches up to a few hundred lost tokens on a 1B-parameter model, and exhibits strong language skew: non-Latin tokens (Korean, Chinese, Thai, Arabic, Hebrew) are disproportionately silenced despite Latin tokens dominating the vocabulary.

- 🎯 Three checks—winning direction, proximity upper bound, exact match—form a certified interval for silenced-token count, collapsing to exact counts at ≥3 bits
- 📊 Silencing appears at 8-bit precision on large-vocabulary models; a 1B-parameter model loses up to a few hundred previously reachable tokens
- 📊 On multilingual vocabularies, silenced tokens are overwhelmingly non-Latin (Korean, Chinese, Thai, Arabic, Hebrew) despite Latin tokens dominating the vocabulary
- ⚠️ Diagnostic tool only—no new quantization algorithm proposed; largest tested model is 1B parameters with no standard downstream benchmarks
